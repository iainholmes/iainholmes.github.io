Resource shocks & local labor markets / 28 October 2026

Coal Demand Shocks Travel Beyond the Mine

Peer-reviewed article · working-paper-version analysis

A new Journal of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists article estimates how falling power-sector demand for thermal coal affected 254 U.S. coal counties. A one-standard-deviation decline in coal-production value per person—$5,127 in the analyzed data—reduced mining employment by 0.94 percentage point in mine records and 1.15 points in labor statistics. Estimated spillovers added roughly 37% to 69% of the direct employment loss, concentrated in transportation and utilities.

Research question and design

Daniel Kraynak stacks county changes over 2007–2012 and 2012–2017. The instrument predicts regional coal demand with a least-cost dispatch model using power-plant operations, state fuel prices, and load, then assigns those shocks to counties through fixed 2002–2007 delivery shares. Commuting-zone controls and region-by-period effects absorb several common shocks. The identifying claim is that modeled regional demand, interacted with predetermined market links, affects local outcomes through coal demand rather than other local labor-market forces.

Economic interpretation

The estimates imply a regional incidence chain: mine payroll falls, linked sectors contract, local tax bases weaken, and households move. Public-school revenue and expenditure each fell about 4%; median home values fell 3.2%; poverty rose; and population declined through lower in-migration. A spatial-equilibrium exercise puts household welfare losses near $0.8–$1.0 billion in central specifications for the 2007–2017 production decline. Those losses are adjustment costs to coal communities, not a tally of the environmental benefits of lower combustion.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

The dispatch instrument supplies stronger leverage than a raw coal-price correlation, and the working paper reports a strong first stage and supporting pre-trend checks. But delivery shares can transmit other regional changes; the dispatch model simplifies congestion and dynamics; Campbell County, Wyoming is excluded as an extreme producer; and inference rests on 254 counties over two periods. Welfare is model-based, uses migration elasticities from other settings, and is sensitive to housing and amenity assumptions. Analysis here uses the complete November 2023 SSRN working paper; the peer-reviewed version was published online on 28 October 2026 and may differ.

Household production & climate policy / 26 October 2026

Clean Equipment and Rooftop Solar Work Together

NBER working paper · calibrated model

Stephie Fried, David Lagakos, and Hannah Rhodenhiser build a household-production model calibrated to a new nationally representative survey and U.S. energy data. For a policy that cuts household emissions 10% at minimum subsidy expenditure, rooftop solar receives the largest subsidy and supplies 38% of abatement. Removing solar from the policy menu raises modeled cost by about 50%; allowing income caps lowers it only about 5%.

Question, data, and model

Households choose equipment and energy for driving, heating, cooling, cooking, and cleaning, plus rooftop solar. They differ in income, roof capacity, and an estimated preference to reduce their own emissions. The calibration matches adoption, equipment spending, energy use, income patterns, and a long-run energy-demand elasticity using the Understanding America Study, Residential Energy Consumption Survey, Consumer Expenditure Survey, and other sources. Solar is valuable because it reduces emissions from every electricity-using task and makes electric equipment cleaner.

Economic interpretation

Income targeting trades inframarginal payments against scale. Excluding richer households avoids subsidizing some adoption that would occur anyway, but reaching a fixed emissions target through fewer eligible buyers requires higher rates. The model also finds that setting environmental preferences to zero raises household emissions 15.6%. Yet those voluntary choices leave a harder-to-persuade margin: the modeled cost of additional policy abatement is 22% higher with the observed preferences than without them.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

This is preliminary, non-peer-reviewed quantitative work. Survey associations help discipline preferences but do not causally identify warm-glow motives; stated motivations may reflect technology ownership or social desirability. Subsidy results are model counterfactuals, not estimated treatment effects, and fiscal expenditure is not a full welfare metric. Grid emissions, landlord–tenant frictions, rebound, manufacturer pricing, network constraints, and distribution within income groups can change the ranking.

Methane regulation & welfare accounting / 29 October 2026

EPA Proposes a Different Methane Ledger

Proposed rule · agency regulatory-impact analysis

EPA proposes to reconsider major parts of its 2024 oil-and-gas methane rule, including the Super Emitter Program and requirements for leak detection, marginal and conventional wells, liquids unloading, controllers, pumps, storage vessels, and associated gas. Relative to the 2024-rule baseline, EPA projects the main option would save industry $2.5 billion per year in compliance expenditure and add $0.2–$0.3 billion in annual energy-market surplus over 2027–2050.

The policy counterfactual

The proposal is not yet final; comments are due 16 December 2026. EPA’s Regulatory Impact Analysis treats the March 2024 rule as the baseline. It projects that the main reconsideration would increase emissions by 60 million short tons of methane, 17 million tons of volatile organic compounds, and 2.3 million tons of hazardous air pollutants through 2050. A co-proposal is less stringent on some well categories and produces still larger projected increases.

Economic interpretation

EPA labels the $2.7–$2.8 billion equivalent annual value an increase in economic surplus because it includes avoided compliance resources, lower consumer prices, higher production, and the foregone value of gas no longer captured. That is not a complete social-benefit estimate. Climate damages from methane, health and ecosystem damages from co-pollutants, and distribution across nearby communities are not netted in the displayed surplus table. A transfer to producers or consumers is also different from a resource saving.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

All quantities are agency projections conditional on equipment assignment, operating costs, production decline, energy-market responses, and baseline compliance. EPA says parts of its model may overstate foregone reductions and requests comment. The estimates therefore compare regulatory scenarios; they do not measure realized emissions, causal effects, or complete net welfare. Final requirements may change after comment.

Power systems & generator investment / 30 October 2026

More New Solar Is Arriving With Batteries

EIA generator inventory · descriptive and planned capacity

EIA reports that more than 16% of utility-scale solar capacity added through September 2026 was co-located with an operating battery, up from 2% in 2017. Reported projects could lift the full-year share to 19%, while plans for 2027 and 2028 put it above 30%. Across the existing U.S. solar fleet, 18% of capacity is now paired with storage.

Why it matters

Co-location can share interconnection infrastructure, store midday output that would otherwise be curtailed or sold at low prices, and shift electricity toward higher-value hours. The pattern is especially strong in California, Arizona, New Mexico, and Nevada, where the co-located share is more than twice the national average. Those regions combine abundant solar output with pronounced hourly price and reliability needs.

Economic interpretation

A battery changes solar’s feasible sales profile, so the technologies are complements even when their private revenues sometimes compete. Co-location can lower transaction and grid-connection costs, but it may also reflect tax rules, queue procedures, capacity accreditation, or developer portfolios rather than underlying system value. Welfare depends on displaced generation, congestion, losses, cycling, market power, and whether shared interconnection delays or accelerates other projects.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

EIA-860M is an operator-reported inventory. Later-year projects can be delayed, resized, canceled, or changed from co-located to stand-alone configurations. The trend is descriptive; it does not identify the causal effect of co-location on cost, reliability, emissions, or interconnection time. Capacity shares are not generation shares, and a battery’s effect depends on when and how it charges.

Emissions measurement & transport policy / 27 October 2026

A New Emissions Model Changes the Baseline

Official model release · regulatory measurement infrastructure

EPA released MOVES6, the emissions model used outside California for state implementation plans and transportation-conformity analyses. The model updates vehicle miles traveled, fleet populations, real-world energy consumption for model years 2017–2024, fuel properties, and several emission-rate errors. It also lowers future electric-vehicle shares relative to MOVES5 and MOVES4 to reflect changed federal standards, incentives, and California truck-policy assumptions.

Why it matters

A model version can alter whether a transportation plan fits within an emissions budget even when the road network does not change. MOVES6 estimates exhaust, evaporative, brake-wear, and tire-wear emissions for criteria pollutants and precursors; it does not estimate re-entrained road dust. States are encouraged to use it promptly for new air plans. A two-year grace period for new transportation-conformity determinations ends 27 October 2028.

Economic interpretation

Regulatory baselines shape incentives and project feasibility. Updated fleet and EV assumptions can change the measured emissions consequence of travel growth, transit, road capacity, or electrification. Yet “latest” is not the same as causally correct: default forecasts combine laws, expected technology adoption, energy use, and travel behavior. Local inputs can improve relevance while also creating discretion and comparability problems.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

MOVES6 is an accounting and simulation system, not an evaluation of a transport policy. EPA says net differences from prior versions depend on place and user inputs. Some plans already far along may continue with earlier versions, and California uses EMFAC. Model outputs inherit uncertainty in travel, fleet turnover, technology, fuel, temperature, and policy; the release notice supplies no general welfare estimate.
