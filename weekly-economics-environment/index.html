Energy economics / 22 September 2026

Electrification’s opportunity is larger than its current market share

Institutional analysis · scenarios

The IEA’s new Electrification report puts electricity at 23% of global final energy consumption in 2025. Its assessment of today’s cost-competitive opportunities implies a 33% share if fully exploited. A separate High Electrification Scenario reaches 35% in 2035. These are distinct comparisons, not three observations on a forecast trend.

Why it matters

This is a consequential way to frame energy security: switching equipment can reduce exposure to recurring fuel purchases, but requires households and firms to commit capital upfront. The economically relevant comparison is the cost of useful services—mobility or heat—not simply the price of a kilowatt-hour.

Economic interpretation

A positive engineering net present value does not guarantee adoption. Financing constraints, landlord–tenant incentives, uncertain future tariffs, and the option value of waiting can all sustain a gap. A well-targeted intervention would address the binding constraint; subsidising every purchase risks paying for adoption that would have happened anyway. Distribution matters too: renters and credit-constrained households may face the largest barriers to capturing operating savings.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

The competitive potential holds other factors constant; it is not a causal estimate of a subsidy’s effect. The 2035 scenario depends on deployment and power-system assumptions. Private cost competitiveness is also different from social welfare once emissions, network costs, and reliability are included.

IEA · Electrification, 22 Sep 2026 ↗
In the research notebook

Which matters more for heat-pump adoption: access to credit or the electricity-to-gas price ratio?

Build a household or regional adoption panel linked to equipment costs, tariffs, and weather. Use a plausibly exogenous eligibility boundary or policy rollout, test pre-trends and anticipation, and account for installer capacity. Estimate marginal emissions using when and where electricity is consumed.

Infrastructure & public investment / 24 September 2026

The grid gets a $5.25 billion investment test

Funding selection · outcomes prospective

DOE announced its intention to support 31 SPARK grid projects with $1.90 billion in federal funding and $3.35 billion in recipient cost sharing. The selected projects are expected to make more than 23 GW of additional grid capacity available. This is planned network capability, not completed generation capacity or an observed fall in electricity bills.

Why it matters

Transmission bottlenecks can prevent low-cost generation from reaching demand. Upgrading an existing corridor can be valuable even without adding a power plant. The week’s IEA grid-modernisation report likewise argues for better use of existing networks alongside expansion.

Economic interpretation

Relieving congestion changes locational prices and redistributes surplus: consumers in a constrained importing area may gain while incumbent generators there lose scarcity rents. Exporting-area prices can rise. Aggregate production-cost savings must be weighed against capital costs, outages during construction, and the costs of public funds. A federal contribution may address benefits that cross utility or state boundaries, but its additionality remains an empirical question.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

Selection is not commissioning, and agency projections are not measured treatment effects. A grant may accelerate an already-planned investment. Emissions can rise or fall depending on which generators change dispatch; transmission capacity alone does not establish a climate benefit.

Read the source
DOE · SPARK selections, 24 Sep 2026 ↗IEA · Modernising Grids, 21 Sep 2026 ↗
In the research notebook

Do reconductoring projects reduce congestion costs—and who receives the savings?

Link actual energisation dates to hourly nodal prices, congestion, dispatch, and outages. Use matched untreated corridors with an event study, allowing for anticipation and staggered treatment. Network spillovers can contaminate “control” nodes, so define electrical exposure rather than relying only on geographic distance.

Climate policy & international trade / 25 September 2026

Carbon-border policy enters a new institutional test

Dispute panel established · no merits ruling

The WTO’s Dispute Settlement Body agreed to Russia’s second request to establish a panel in dispute DS639. The challenge concerns the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism and an alleged export subsidy associated with the Emissions Trading System. The EU expressed confidence in WTO consistency. Establishing a panel does not decide the merits or itself repeal the measures.

Why it matters

The durability of carbon-pricing arrangements affects expectations for long-lived industrial investment. Producers making decisions about cleaner steel, cement, or other traded goods care about the expected policy path as well as today’s carbon price.

Economic interpretation

A border adjustment seeks to reduce incentives to relocate emissions-intensive production when domestic and foreign carbon costs differ. Its incidence depends on trade elasticities, market power, and how easily buyers switch suppliers. Some burden may fall on foreign producers through lower net prices; some may reach EU downstream users. Administrative requirements can create a disproportionate fixed cost for smaller exporters. Redirecting cleaner output toward Europe can also change measured import intensity without reducing total global emissions.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

The WTO announcement establishes a procedural development. It supplies neither a legal verdict nor an estimate of leakage, consumer-price effects, or emissions reductions. Anticipated litigation and simultaneous trade policies make a single announcement-date comparison weak causal evidence.

Read the source
WTO · DSB meeting report, 25 Sep 2026 ↗WTO · DS639 case record ↗
In the research notebook

Does carbon-border exposure induce cleaner production or redirect existing trade?

Construct product–country exposure using pre-policy trade and emissions intensity. Compare covered and comparable uncovered products over time, testing differential pre-trends. Track third-market exports and total production as well as EU imports; otherwise diversion can be mistaken for abatement.

Research · urban & environmental economics / 21 September 2026

Insurance stress has an urban geography of its own

Working paper · observational evidence

Matthew E. Kahn and Wesley A. Miller’s Hutchins Center Working Paper 115 builds a Local Insurance Strain Index from affordability, availability, and insurer loss ratios, using 2018–2022 data. Within metropolitan areas, strain is greatest near urban centres even as measured natural-hazard exposure tends to rise farther out. Brookings released the research on 21 September; the underlying observations are historical.

Why it matters

Insurance connects physical risk, housing affordability, and household location choices. Pooling environmental and other insured risks into one premium can obscure which policy problem needs attention.

Economic interpretation

The relevant framework is spatial equilibrium with risk priced into the user cost of housing. Higher expected ownership costs may be capitalised into lower sale prices; common neighbourhood disamenities may also depress rents. Public catastrophe backstops can shift who ultimately bears environmental risk. Similar private-market loss ratios across locations do not, on their own, prove that market power is absent: expenses, reinsurance, coverage, and the risks outside the private sample matter.

Magnitude, with a sample warning. In the common sample with both price and rent data, one standard deviation of insurance strain is associated with 10.6% lower home prices and 3.1% lower rents. The home-price association is 5.5% in the broader price sample. This sensitivity is a reason to inspect selection, not treat the larger coefficient as a universal effect.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

The preferred regressions use metropolitan-area × year fixed effects, identifying comparisons across ZIP codes in the same metro and year—not random insurance shocks. Unobserved building quality, crime, and neighbourhood demand can confound the relationship. Texas and less-populated areas are important coverage limitations. The paper is a working paper, not a verified causal finding.

Read the source
Kahn & Miller · Working Paper 115, pp. 5–10 & Appendix A ↗Brookings · Research release, 21 Sep 2026 ↗
In the research notebook

How much insurance-cost change is capitalised into housing prices after an external insurer shock?

Start with the paper’s Federal Insurance Office data, ACS income, hazard measures, and housing outcomes. For a causal extension, investigate insurer exits driven by losses elsewhere, using pre-existing local insurer shares. The exclusion restriction must rule out correlated local hazards and insurer sorting; test both explicitly.

05
Agriculture & environmental regulation / 22 September 2026

Glyphosate review makes the value of information concrete

Research coordination · review ongoing

HHS and EPA announced a memorandum to exchange evidence and coordinate research for EPA’s glyphosate registration review. Priorities include formulations, occupational epidemiology, and exposure measurement. EPA retains registration authority; the agreement creates no new authority and does not predetermine the review. The announcement anticipates an updated human-health assessment in early 2027.

Why it matters

The substantive development is a change in the evidence-building process for regulation, with potential implications for a widely used agricultural input. It is not a new prohibition or a new finding of harm or safety.

Economic interpretation

This is a value-of-information problem: additional evidence is useful when it can change the preferred action enough to justify its cost and delay. Economic assessment should examine farmer productivity, worker and bystander exposure, and substitution into other chemicals or cultivation practices. A narrow analysis of one active ingredient can miss changes in total risk, soil disturbance, input costs, and who bears them. Benefits and burdens may fall on different people.

What the evidence does—and does not—say

An interagency agreement does not establish a health effect. Exposure is not randomly assigned: crop choice, occupation, co-exposures, and protective practices complicate inference. Evidence about an active ingredient should not automatically be transferred to every commercial formulation. No health-effect estimate is inferred here.

Read the source
HHS–EPA · Partnership announcement, 22 Sep 2026 ↗
In the research notebook

How would farmers substitute if future use conditions changed, and with what welfare consequences?

Develop a prospective design linking crop-level pesticide use, yields, input spending, and local exposure measures. If a policy changes, examine treatment intensity rather than a simple national before–after comparison. Pre-specify substitution outcomes and distributional groups before estimating benefits and costs.

Value of information · substitution · benefit–cost analysis
The synthesis / Editorial interpretation

What connects this week

The common thread is the difference between an attractive technology or policy and the institutions needed to make it work. Electrification requires finance and usable networks. Grid investment requires coordination across jurisdictions. Carbon-border incentives depend on credible, administrable rules. Insurance prices reflect both physical exposure and the way society pools losses. Pesticide decisions depend on what the evidence can actually resolve.

Three economic ideas carry across the five readings: complementarity between investments, incidence across households and firms, and the value of information under uncertainty. A cheaper technology can deliver little welfare gain if a complementary input is missing. A policy can improve aggregate efficiency while imposing concentrated losses. A new measurement can be valuable before any new rule is adopted.

For research, the challenge is to distinguish a changed constraint from a coincident trend. Match each empirical design to the mechanism: network exposure for transmission, trade diversion for carbon borders, omitted neighbourhood characteristics for insurance, and substitution for agricultural regulation. Announcements establish questions; credible counterfactuals establish effects.

This week’s research question: when we reduce one cost, where does the risk—and the bill—move next?

Methods & concepts notebook

A small index for returning to the economic ideas behind the week.

Complementarity, adoption & capital constraints

Equipment, finance, skills, and network access can be complementary inputs. A subsidy’s marginal effect depends on which constraint binds. Return to electrification and grid investment. Distinguish engineering cost potential from observed willingness and ability to adopt.

Incidence, leakage & equilibrium spillovers

Statutory responsibility does not identify who ultimately bears a cost. Elasticities and market structure determine price pass-through. Spillovers also threaten empirical controls: adjacent grid nodes and third-country export markets can be indirectly treated. Return to carbon borders and transmission.

Fixed effects, hedonic prices & selection

Metropolitan-area × year fixed effects remove shocks shared within a metro and year; they do not remove all ZIP-level confounding. Housing prices can reflect the present value of insurance costs and the underlying disamenities that produce those costs. Return to the working paper. Keep the 2018–2022 observation window separate from the 2026 release date.

Value of information & behavioural substitution

The expected value of information is the gain from making a better decision after learning, relative to deciding with current information. Compare that gain with research costs and delay. A benefit–cost analysis should include substitutions and affected groups, not assume the regulated input simply disappears. Return to glyphosate review.
