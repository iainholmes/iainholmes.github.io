01

Complementarity

One input raises the value of another. Equipment, skills, credit and network access can work together, so the return to one investment depends on the availability of the others.

Why it matters

A low equipment price may produce little adoption when finance or usable infrastructure is missing. Coordinated investment can change which constraint binds.

A caution

Do not infer complementarity from investments merely appearing together. A binding constraint and a causal interaction need evidence.

02

Adoption & capital constraints

A technology can be cost-effective over its lifetime while remaining unaffordable upfront. Adoption depends on liquidity, borrowing terms, uncertainty and the costs of changing equipment.

Why it matters

A subsidy, loan or tariff reform affects different margins. Their welfare effects depend on which households can respond and what emissions their use displaces.

A caution

Engineering cost potential is neither observed willingness to adopt nor a forecast. Financing constraints must be distinguished from preferences and installation barriers.

03

Incidence

Incidence describes who ultimately bears a cost or receives a benefit after prices, wages and quantities adjust. The person legally required to pay need not bear the whole economic burden.

Why it matters

Elasticities, market power and the time available to adjust determine pass-through. Distributional analysis follows the burden through households and firms.

A caution

Do not identify incidence from statutory liability alone. Short-run and long-run burdens may differ, and administrative costs can fall unevenly.

04

Leakage & equilibrium spillovers

Leakage occurs when a policy moves an activity or its emissions elsewhere. Equilibrium spillovers arise when treatment changes prices or opportunities faced by people outside the directly treated group.

Why it matters

Grid upgrades affect connected nodes; carbon-border incentives affect third-market trade. Both welfare accounting and empirical comparisons need those indirect effects.

A caution

A geographically distant control may still be economically exposed. Cleaner imports do not establish lower global emissions if production is simply redirected.

05

Fixed effects

Fixed effects remove the variation shared by units in a specified group. Metro-by-year effects compare neighbourhoods within the same metro and year, absorbing shocks common to that group.

Why it matters

They can help isolate variation appropriate to an empirical question and separate common conditions from local differences. The remaining variation determines the interpretation of the coefficient.

A caution

Fixed effects do not make the remaining variation randomly assigned. Unobserved neighbourhood conditions can still affect both insurance strain and housing prices.

06

Hedonic prices

A hedonic framework treats a differentiated good’s price as reflecting a bundle of attributes. Housing prices can capitalise expected insurance costs, amenities and local risks.

Why it matters

Changes in ownership costs can shift demand and sale prices. The framework connects environmental conditions and market costs to household location choices.

A caution

A price association is not automatically willingness to pay or a causal effect. Correlated attributes, sorting and equilibrium adjustment complicate that interpretation.

07

Selection

Selection means the observed people, places or outcomes enter a sample or comparison through a process related to the question being studied. That process can change both estimates and their reach.

Why it matters

Insurance results differ between the common price-and-rent sample and the broader home-price sample. Coverage and composition matter when drawing conclusions about housing markets.

A caution

A larger sample reduces sampling noise but does not automatically remove selection bias. Compare coverage, inclusion rules and plausible missing outcomes.

08

Value of information

The expected value of information is the improvement in a decision made after learning, relative to the best decision available with current knowledge. Net value subtracts research costs and the cost of delay.

Why it matters

Measurement can be economically useful before a new rule is adopted, particularly when evidence could change which action is preferred or how it targets exposure.

A caution

More precise information is not necessarily valuable enough to collect. Ask which decision can change; an agreement to study a risk is not a finding of harm or safety.

09

Behavioural substitution

People adjust to changed prices, rules and constraints by choosing alternatives. Removing one input does not imply that its associated activity disappears.

Why it matters

Farmers may use other chemicals or cultivation practices. A benefit–cost analysis must account for changes in yields, total exposure, soil disturbance and who bears the costs.

A caution

Do not evaluate a regulated input in isolation or assume alternatives have zero cost or risk. Specify plausible substitutes before estimating net benefits.

10

Counterfactual reasoning

A counterfactual describes what would have happened to the outcome without the intervention being evaluated. An effect is the difference between the observed policy path and that credible alternative path.

Why it matters

Adoption, investment and prices can change even without a new policy. The baseline trajectory separates policy additionality from changes that would have occurred anyway.

A caution

A before–after difference, engineering ceiling or agency projection is not by itself a credible counterfactual. Anticipation and concurrent changes require attention.

11

Identification

Identification asks whether the available evidence and assumptions can distinguish the effect or mechanism of interest from competing explanations. It begins with the source of variation and the comparison being made.

Why it matters

Proposed designs must match the mechanism: network exposure, product–country trade exposure, insurer shocks or treatment intensity. Each comparison needs assumptions that can be explained and examined.

A caution

A sophisticated regression does not supply its own research design. Announcements, correlations and procedural changes cannot alone establish causal effects.

12

Fiscal multipliers & local projections

A fiscal response depends on what is financed, how it is financed and the state of the economy. Local projections estimate an outcome’s response separately at each horizon after a specified shock; a response of GDP-per-capita growth is not automatically a level-output multiplier.

Why it matters

Environmental spending can draw on idle resources or compete for scarce capacity. Its short-run growth response and its environmental or welfare benefits are different objects, and averages can hide differences across countries and spending categories.

A caution

Residual-based shocks are not randomized spending. Their interpretation requires credible restrictions on anticipation and concurrent shocks, comparable fiscal measurement and suitable inference. Do not turn the reported growth response into a universal multiplier.

13

Spatial equilibrium & migration

Households and firms respond to differences in productivity, amenities and costs across locations. Migration, trade and investment adjust those differences, subject to moving costs and other frictions; a spatial equilibrium describes the resulting allocation rather than a fixed map of exposure.

Why it matters

The AMOC counterfactual changes both where losses occur and how mobility cushions them. The landslide reading shows why moving people is not equivalent to costless adaptation: productive networks and destination conditions also matter.

A caution

A model with restricted mobility is a conditional comparison, not an observed migration effect. Aggregate gains from relocation need not imply that displaced households recover, and estimates from one setting need not transport to another.

14

Private returns & social value

An investment’s private revenue and its net social value can diverge when prices redistribute surplus or decisions impose costs and benefits on others. Social accounting follows resource costs and external effects, distinguishes transfers from new surplus, and avoids counting the same benefit twice.

Why it matters

Storage can compress the price spreads that fund its own entry while benefiting consumers. Public nuclear finance adds system benefits and taxpayer risk to the accounting. Climate liability asks whether institutions can shift external costs into private incentives.

A caution

A positive consumer-surplus change is not a complete welfare calculation or a sufficient subsidy case. Market rules, ownership, financing additionality, transaction costs and the marginal alternative matter; model results are conditional rather than universal.

15

Financing wedges & subsidy equivalents

Tax rules and public credit alter the effective cost of capital. A financing wedge changes relative investment incentives; a loan’s subsidy equivalent is its advantage over a credible market-financing benchmark after accounting for repayment terms and risk, not its face value.

Why it matters

Debt deductibility can favor carbon-intensive firms through differences in leverage. A conditional nuclear loan can shift borrowing costs and default exposure between plant owners and taxpayers. Both require separating financing effects from the project’s underlying social value.

A caution

A debt-tax reform is not the same instrument as a carbon price. A loan announcement without interest, maturity, collateral and default information does not identify its subsidy equivalent, and neither announcement establishes investment additionality.

16

Participation constraints

An agreement is individually acceptable only if each participant receives at least the payoff available under its relevant outside option. Transfers and differentiated obligations can enlarge the set of acceptable agreements without changing the underlying physical externality.

Why it matters

The climate-cooperation model connects carbon-tax incidence, WTO market access and compensation. An agreement that raises total welfare can still fail when a member’s terms-of-trade loss makes defection preferable.

A caution

The feasible policy set depends on the disagreement point, enforcement and what governments can observe and transfer. A calibrated unanimously acceptable carbon price is a conditional model result, not a forecast of political agreement.

17

Spatial boundary comparisons

A narrow spatial comparison uses nearby locations on opposite sides of an exposure boundary to seek a credible comparison group. Identification depends on other determinants of outcomes being sufficiently comparable across that boundary, not on proximity alone.

Why it matters

The landslide study compares households inside precise slide paths with nearby households on the same slopes and similar modeled risk. Balance, alternative comparison groups, selection checks and spatial inference support the exposure comparison.

A caution

Disaster exposure is not randomized. Indirect effects can reach nearby controls, retrospective outcomes can be imperfect, and a credible total exposure effect does not separately identify displacement or social-capital mechanisms.

18

Location-specific social capital

Local relationships can support production, employment contacts, reciprocal help and informal insurance. These networks are a form of location-specific capital: replacing a physical asset or moving to a safer place need not replace the relationships that made livelihoods viable.

Why it matters

Following displaced households reveals losses missed by surveying only the original disaster location. Recovery policy may need to preserve or rebuild community ties as well as provide housing and monetary assistance.

A caution

An association between networks and recovery is not a separately identified causal effect of social capital. Placement, destination quality and household selection can confound it; the published reading distinguishes the stronger exposure design from less cleanly identified mechanisms.
